January 2020. A crypto exchange posts a portrait of Satoshi Nakamoto, drawn as a maze of lines. "2.1 BTC are hidden in this picture, can you find the solution?" Two months, thousands of people, a GitHub repo, a Telegram group. Nobody gets it.

Then the designer explains it. A prime from e, two words, two Base58 alphabets, little endian. Honestly? Nobody was getting that one from the picture alone.

## How the Phemex 2.1 BTC puzzle works

### A portrait, an address and a split prize

The [rules page](https://phemex.com/announcements/try-to-solve-our-2-btc-puzzle) gives you the picture and the address `1h8BNZkhsPiu6EKazP19WkGxDw3jHf9aT`. The 2.1 BTC is two prizes. 1.1 BTC sits on the address. Take the key and move it. The other 1 BTC lands on your Phemex account, once you prove you hold the key.

So the record says 1.1 BTC. That's what the chain can show. The extra coin was a promise on an exchange account, not a UTXO.

Why would an exchange do this? The page says it's about key custody. It name drops its own HD cold wallet system and quotes the CEO, Jack Tao. A marketing stunt with a real key in it. Fair enough.

### Max's letter and the hints

A week later came [A Letter from Max](https://phemex.com/announcements/a-letter-from-max). Max introduces himself as a Phemex co-founder and "the satoshi-maze puzzle designer". The letter has five hints:

- The first 21-digit prime in consecutive digits of e is `957496696762772407663`. He just gives it away.
- The key is a big integer, not a WIF.
- The file name doesn't matter.
- Some words from the portrait, "without I/O", become a 27-digit number.
- "Go back to step 4) again if you can't figure it out".

The letter also fixes the winner. Whoever moves the 1.1 BTC first wins.

### "little is big", twice

On February 21 came [the last hint](https://phemex.com/announcements/the-last-hint-of-phemex-2-1btc-puzzle). The Goddess Pheme repeated the words "little is big", twice within three days. Same page, a deadline. No solve by March 21 and they publish the full solution.

Little endian, read twice. Obvious after the fact, right?

## The published solution

### Three numbers, one product

The [Puzzle Ending](https://web.archive.org/web/20200529172409/https://phemex.com/references/articles/puzzle-ending) went up on March 21, 2020. Phemex doesn't serve it anymore, so the record cites the Wayback capture. Here's the recipe:

1. The prime from e, `957496696762772407663`.
2. `SatoshiNakamoto` decoded with the Bitcoin Base58 alphabet. The bytes `672c5725fa8fc1aa52c3c4`, read little endian, give `237871847045914904726285415`. That's the 27-digit number.
3. `Phemex` decoded with the XRP alphabet, `rpshnaf39w…`. The bytes `035f251581`, little endian, give `554405551875`.
4. Multiply all three. The product is the private key, `0x141dc7bec50472bb381be8e18f6d6b397773d71fc5d91d41fb`.

The 27-digit number starts with 23. The first hint came out on January 23. Max points that out himself. Cute.

### Why "little is big"

Both Base58 numbers are bigger read little endian than big endian. That's the whole hint. Big endian gives `124728751148945267645137860` and `14481167745`, and neither leads anywhere.

And the XRP alphabet? Decode `Phemex` with the Bitcoin one and you get `03781a18d1` instead. Same letters, other bytes. Look at the portrait again. "First 21-digit prime found in consecutive digits of e" runs along the hair. ETH, XRP, BTC and Phemex sit in the maze around the face. How the paths tie them together, the Ending doesn't say.

### Check it yourself

Every step reruns. `@agntn/encodings` decodes both words, BigInt does the rest. `puzzles verify satoshi-maze` derives the compressed key and lands on the address. The key on the record is padded to 64 hex digits, nothing else changed.

## What happened to the 1.1 BTC

### Out 16 minutes before the ending

The address got a 10,000 sat test on January 16, 2020. It spent 7,900 of it two hours later. That spend put the public key on chain the same morning. At 06:53 UTC came the 1.1 BTC. The rules page went up at 08:24.

On March 21 at 02:31 UTC, 1.099 BTC left in one transaction. The Ending went up at 02:47. It says "with the release of this information, the prize money will no longer be available". The Ending doesn't name a txid. But nobody had won, and the money went 16 minutes before the key came out. So the record calls that spend a `decrease`, the author taking the prize back.

### The dust, swept the same morning

In between, people sent the address 84 tiny payments. 53 of them are exactly 666 sat. Somebody also dropped 30,000 sat on January 22. With the 90,000 sat change from the withdrawal, 198,300 sat were left. At 05:17 UTC all of it went out in one transaction. The key had been public for two and a half hours. Anyone could have done it, so the record calls it a `sweep`.

Nothing is left on the address. `status` is `expired`, the same as the [book quiz](/collections/book-quiz). The prize went home, then the answer came out.

### Four stages, each with its answer

The stages follow the Ending, one line each. The prime, the 27-digit number, the last number, the product. Every stage carries its line from the Ending as its `answer`, dated March 21.

### Hints from four pages

The record keeps :dataset-count{of="hints" puzzle="satoshi-maze"} hints, all from Phemex. The rules, Max's letter, the last hint and the Ending. Each one quotes its page word for word. The old pages moved from `/references/articles/` to `/announcements/`, so each `confirmation` points at the 2020 capture of the old address.

### The picture

`assets/satoshi-maze/puzzle.png` is the portrait. Phemex still serves those bytes from its image CDN. The Wayback copy from April 2020 has the same SHA-256, so the file hasn't changed since the hunt.
